**Part A: Narrative Transcript**

**Opening & Warm-Up**

**Q: Tell me about your role caring for your mother.**

> I'm the everything person. My mum Gloria is seventy-four --- COPD, arthritis, anxiety, nine medications daily. She lives alone, twenty minutes from me. My brother's in London, my sister's in Auckland. They call occasionally. I visit every other day, manage all the medical appointments, coordinate the pharmacy, deal with the physio. I'm a part-time teacher so my schedule is flexible-ish, but 'flexible' just means I do two jobs instead of one.

**Block 1: Falls**

**Q: Walk me through the last time your mother had a fall.**

> Six weeks ago. Bathroom. She slipped getting out of the shower and fractured her wrist. Her medical alert necklace was dead --- she'd forgotten to charge it for three days. I found out because she managed to crawl from the bathroom to the hallway table, pull the phone down by the cord, and call me. Ninety minutes after the fall.
>
> The physio told me afterwards: 'The next fracture could be a hip. And that's a different conversation entirely.' I knew what she meant. A hip fracture at seventy-four with COPD --- that's a hospital stay, potential pneumonia, maybe she never comes home.

***[Probe]** What information did you wish you had?*

> That the necklace was dead. Something that tells ME when the battery is low --- not her, me. She forgot. She'll always forget. The system should know that and tell the person who can actually do something about it.

**Block 2: Medications**

**Q: Tell me about managing nine medications.**

> Nine medications. Three times a day. Two of them can't be taken together. One has to be taken with food, one on an empty stomach. I have a university degree and I can barely keep track. I made a colour-coded chart in Excel --- it takes me forty-five minutes to print and laminate a new one each week. And the pharmacy auto-refill is my most reliable tool, but it only tells me medications were picked up, not taken. The gap between dispensed and swallowed is where everything goes wrong.

**Q: What would 'perfectly managed' look like?**

> One place where I can see: did she take her pills, has she moved around today, is anyone else checking on her today. One place, not five apps and a binder. I carry a physical binder of medical records to every appointment. The binder weighs more than my handbag. It shouldn't be this hard.

**Block 3: Communication & Escalation**

**Q: How do you decide when something is an emergency versus watch-and-wait?**

> I don't know. That's the honest answer. I called 000 for the fall because obviously. But last month she had a dizzy spell and I drove over and she was fine --- dehydration. Before that she was short of breath for two days and I didn't take her in because I thought it was the COPD, but the GP later said it was a mild chest infection and we should have come in sooner. I'm guessing every time. I don't know what's an emergency and what's just being seventy-four.

**Block 5: Willingness to Prioritize**

**Q: How much time per week do you spend on care coordination?**

> Ten hours, easily. Plus the mental load, which never stops. Every time my phone rings after nine PM, my heart stops. Every single time. That's not a way to live.
>
> I pay sixty-five dollars a month for the medical alert necklace. Thirty a month on printed charts and binder supplies. I'd pay a hundred and eighty a month for something reliable. I evaluated a fall detection watch last year --- too many false alarms, mum stopped wearing it after three weeks.

**Wrap-Up**

**Q: Who else should I talk to?**

> The pharmacist at the chemist near mum's place --- she knows more about mum's actual medication habits than anyone. And my friend Kate who works in aged care. She's seen the inside of the system from the other side.
